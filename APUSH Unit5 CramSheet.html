5.1

Contextualizing Period 5

  • Opens with Manifest Destiny-driven expansion (1844); closes with the end of Reconstruction (1877).

5.2

Manifest Destiny

  • Belief Americans were destined to expand coast to coast.
  • Reinforced by economic motives: new farmland, Pacific trade access.
  • Expansion intensified conflict with Native nations and Mexico.
Manifest Destiny

5.3

The Mexican-American War

  • Sparked by the U.S. annexation of Texas (1845) and a border dispute.
  • Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848): Mexico cedes the Mexican Cession (California, Southwest).
  • This vast new territory immediately reignited the slavery debate.

5.4

The Compromise of 1850

  • California admitted as a free state.
  • Popular sovereignty in Utah & New Mexico.
  • Stronger Fugitive Slave Law — deeply unpopular in the North.
  • Slave trade (not slavery) banned in Washington D.C.

5.5

Sectional Conflict: Regional Differences

  • North: free-labor, industrial economy; growing opposition to slavery's expansion.
  • South: cotton-dependent economy; defends slavery as essential to its way of life.
  • South fears losing political power as the North's population (and House seats) grow faster.
Free labor
Diverging regional identities.
5.6

Failure of Compromise

  • Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854): popular sovereignty reopens the debate, repeals the Missouri Compromise line.
  • “Bleeding Kansas”: violent conflict between pro-/anti-slavery settlers.
  • Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857): enslaved people are not citizens; Congress can't ban slavery in territories.
  • John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry (1859).
Kansas-Nebraska ActDred Scott
Trap: Dred Scott went further than just one case — it ruled Congress had no power to ban slavery in any territory.

5.7

Election of 1860 & Secession

  • Democratic Party splits sectionally → Lincoln wins with minimal Southern support.
  • South Carolina secedes first (Dec. 1860); Confederate States of America forms.
  • War begins at Fort Sumter (April 1861).

5.8

Military Conflict in the Civil War

  • Antietam (1862, bloodiest day) → opens door to Emancipation Proclamation.
  • Gettysburg (1863): major turning point, repels Lee's invasion.
  • Sherman's March (1864) → Lee surrenders at Appomattox (1865).

5.9

Government Policies During the Civil War

  • Emancipation Proclamation (1863): frees enslaved people in Confederate territory, reframes the war.
  • Military draft, national banking system, income tax expand federal power.
  • Lincoln controversially suspends habeas corpus in some areas.

5.10

Reconstruction

  • 13th (1865): abolishes slavery. 14th (1868): citizenship & equal protection. 15th (1870): Black men's voting rights.
  • Federal troops enforce these new rights in the South.

5.11

Failures of Reconstruction

  • Black Codes & later Jim Crow restrict new freedoms; KKK violence suppresses Black political participation.
  • Compromise of 1877: federal troops withdraw, ending Reconstruction.

5.12

Comparison in Period 5

  • 1844: slavery contested but legal; limited federal power.
  • 1877: slavery abolished; federal power expanded; but racial inequality persists in practice.

⚠

Commonly Missed on the Exam

  • The Emancipation Proclamation only freed enslaved people in Confederate-held territory, not border states.
  • Popular sovereignty appears twice: 1850 (Utah/NM) and 1854 (Kansas/Nebraska) — know which context.
  • Compromise of 1850 vs. Compromise of 1877: completely different events, 27 years apart.
  • Bleeding Kansas is a direct result of the Kansas-Nebraska Act, not a separate unrelated event.
  • Radical Reconstruction's amendments were legally strong, but enforcement collapsed after 1877.
  • Distinguish Black Codes (Reconstruction-era) from Jim Crow laws (came later, more entrenched).
