1.1

Structure of Water & Hydrogen Bonding

Key Points

  • Water is polar: O has higher electronegativity → pulls shared electrons closer → δ⁻ on O, δ⁺ on H.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between the δ⁺ H of one water molecule and the δ⁻ O of another — weak individually, powerful in numbers.
  • Cohesion = water sticking to itself (H-bonds). Adhesion = water sticking to other polar surfaces.
  • High specific heat: H-bonds absorb a lot of energy before temperature rises → stabilizes aquatic environments & body temperature.
  • High heat of vaporization: evaporative cooling (e.g., sweating) removes a lot of heat.
  • Ice floats: H-bonds lock water into a spaced-out lattice when frozen → ice is less dense than liquid water.
  • Water is the "universal solvent" — polar/ionic substances dissolve readily in it.
PolarityHydrogen bondCohesionAdhesionSpecific heatSolvent
Trap: Hydrogen bonds are intermolecular (between molecules) — don't confuse them with the covalent bonds holding each water molecule's own atoms together.

1.2

Elements of Life

Key Points

  • Most common elements in living things: C, H, O, N (remember "CHON"), plus smaller but essential amounts of P and S.
  • Carbon has 4 valence electrons → forms up to 4 covalent bonds → builds chains, branches, and rings → the "backbone" of organic molecules.
  • Covalent bond: atoms share electrons (strong).
  • Ionic bond: electrons transferred, forming oppositely charged ions that attract.
  • Polar covalent: unequal sharing → partial charges. Nonpolar covalent: equal sharing.
  • N is essential for amino acids & nucleotide bases. P is essential for phospholipids, ATP, and the DNA/RNA backbone.

1.3

Introduction to Macromolecules

Key Points

  • Monomers (small repeating units) link to form polymers (large chains).
  • Dehydration synthesis (condensation): joins 2 monomers, releases one H₂O. Builds polymers.
  • Hydrolysis: adds one H₂O to break a bond between monomers. Breaks polymers apart (this is how digestion works).
  • The 4 major macromolecule classes: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
MonomerPolymerDehydration synthesisHydrolysis
Trap: Dehydration synthesis releases water (builds); hydrolysis uses water (breaks). Students often mix up which direction adds vs. removes water.
Dehydration synthesis builds polymers by releasing water; hydrolysis breaks them apart by adding water.

1.4

Carbohydrates

Key Points

  • Monomer: monosaccharide (e.g., glucose, fructose, galactose).
  • Starch — plant storage polysaccharide, α-glycosidic bonds, digestible by humans.
  • Glycogen — animal storage polysaccharide, highly branched for fast glucose mobilization.
  • Cellulose — plant structural polysaccharide (cell walls), β-glycosidic bonds, not digestible by most animals.
  • Chitin — structural polysaccharide in fungal cell walls & arthropod exoskeletons.
MonosaccharideDisaccharidePolysaccharideStarchGlycogenCellulose
Trap: Starch and cellulose are both glucose polymers — the only structural difference is α vs. β bonds, which is why humans can digest one but not the other.
Starch (coiled), glycogen (branched), and cellulose (straight, fibrous) are all glucose polymers.
1.5

Lipids

Key Points

  • Lipids are hydrophobic — long nonpolar hydrocarbon chains repel water.
  • Triglyceride = glycerol + 3 fatty acids → long-term energy storage.
  • Saturated fat: no double bonds → straight chains → pack tightly → solid at room temp.
  • Unsaturated fat: ≥1 double bond → kinked chains → pack loosely → liquid at room temp.
  • Phospholipids are amphipathic (polar head + nonpolar tails) → spontaneously form the bilayer of cell membranes.
  • Steroids (e.g., cholesterol) have a 4-fused-ring structure — hormones & membrane components.

1.6

Nucleic Acids

Key Points

  • Monomer: nucleotide = nitrogenous base + pentose sugar + phosphate group.
  • Nucleotides link via dehydration synthesis → sugar-phosphate backbone.
  • DNA: sugar = deoxyribose; double-stranded; bases A-T, C-G; stores genetic info long-term.
  • RNA: sugar = ribose; single-stranded; bases A-U, C-G (uracil replaces thymine); used in protein synthesis.
  • Purines (A, G) = double-ring. Pyrimidines (C, T, U) = single-ring.
NucleotideNitrogenous basePurinePyrimidineComplementary base pairing
Trap: RNA uses uracil, not thymine — a very commonly tested substitution.
DNA base pairing: A–T and C–G, held by hydrogen bonds between two antiparallel backbones.
1.7

Proteins

Key Points

  • Monomer: amino acid = central C + amino group + carboxyl group + H + variable R group (determines chemical identity).
  • Amino acids link via a peptide bond (dehydration synthesis).
  • Primary structure: linear amino acid sequence.
  • Secondary structure: local folding (α-helix, β-pleated sheet) from H-bonds along the backbone.
  • Tertiary structure: overall 3D shape from R-group interactions.
  • Quaternary structure: multiple polypeptide chains joined (e.g., hemoglobin).
  • Denaturation: heat/pH/salt disrupts bonds → protein unfolds → loses function (primary structure usually stays intact).
Amino acidPeptide bondPrimarySecondaryTertiaryQuaternaryDenaturation
Trap: A single amino acid substitution in primary structure (like in sickle cell hemoglobin) can cascade into major changes at every higher level.
The four levels of protein structure: primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary.

⚠

Commonly Missed on the Exam

  • Direction confusion: dehydration synthesis releases water and builds polymers; hydrolysis uses water and breaks polymers.
  • Hydrogen bonds vs. covalent bonds: H-bonds are weak, intermolecular attractions — not the strong covalent bonds within a single water molecule.
  • RNA's uracil: replaces thymine — don't write "T" when describing RNA base pairing.
  • Starch vs. cellulose: same monomer (glucose), different bond type (α vs. β) — this single fact explains digestibility.
  • Saturated ≠ healthy default: "saturated" refers to bond structure (no C=C double bonds), not a value judgment — know the structural reason for solid vs. liquid at room temp.
  • R group defines the amino acid, not the amino/carboxyl/central carbon (those are identical in all 20 amino acids).
  • Primary structure changes cascade: one amino acid swap can alter secondary → tertiary → quaternary structure and function (e.g., sickle cell).
  • Denaturation ≠ digestion: denaturation unfolds a protein (breaks weak bonds); it does not break peptide bonds the way hydrolysis/digestion does.
