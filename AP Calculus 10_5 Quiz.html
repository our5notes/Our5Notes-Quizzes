A student claims that since the terms of the harmonic series (1/n) approach 0, the series must converge, just very slowly. What is the flaw in this reasoning?

B. The harmonic series famously diverges despite its terms shrinking to zero, illustrating that this condition alone never guarantees convergence.

(a) Classify each as convergent or divergent: (i) Σ1/n^2.5 (ii) Σ1/n^0.9 (iii) Σ1/n
Sample response: (i) p=2.5>1, converges. (ii) p=0.9≤1, diverges. (iii) p=1, diverges (the harmonic series).
(b) Explain why p=1 (the harmonic series) is the critical boundary case between convergence and divergence for p-series.
Sample response: At exactly p=1, the terms shrink just slowly enough that the partial sums still grow without bound (confirmed by the Integral Test, since ∫1/x dx=ln(x)→∞); for any p slightly greater than 1, the terms shrink fast enough for convergence.
(c) Without directly computing, explain whether Σ1/n^1.001 converges or diverges, and why this might seem surprising.
Sample response: It converges, since p=1.001>1, satisfying the p-series convergence condition — even though p is extremely close to 1, the series technically still converges, which can seem surprising given how close it is to the divergent boundary case.
