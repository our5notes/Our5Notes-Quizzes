2.1

Contextualizing Period 2

Key Points

  • Period 2 begins with Jamestown (1607), the first permanent English settlement.
  • England, Spain, France, and the Netherlands established competing colonies for profit, religious freedom, and geopolitical rivalry.
  • Colonial societies developed distinct regional identities shaped by climate, labor, and founders' goals.
  • The period closes with the outbreak of the French and Indian War (1754).

2.2

European Colonization

Key Points

  • Spain: mission system to convert & control Native peoples (Florida, Southwest).
  • France: fur trade, closer alliances with Native nations, smaller population.
  • England: largest colonial population; mix of profit-driven joint-stock companies and religious refuge; generally displaced Native peoples.
  • Netherlands: New Netherland, centered on the Hudson River fur trade.

2.3

The Regions of British Colonies

Key Points

  • New England: rocky soil → fishing, trade, small farms; tight-knit Puritan towns.
  • Middle Colonies: fertile land, grain exports (“breadbasket”), ethnically diverse.
  • Chesapeake: tobacco plantations, indentured → enslaved labor.
  • Lower South: rice & indigo, large plantations, enslaved majority.

2.4

Transatlantic Trade

Key Points

  • Manufactured goods (Europe → Africa), enslaved people (Africa → colonies, the Middle Passage), raw materials (colonies → Europe).
  • Operated under mercantilism: colonies exist to enrich the mother country.
  • Enforced by the Navigation Acts, restricting colonial trade to English ships.
Middle PassageMercantilismNavigation Acts
Trap: Know the direction of each leg — goods to Africa, enslaved people to the colonies, raw materials back to Europe.

2.5

Interactions Between American Indians and Europeans

Key Points

  • Interactions ranged from trade (furs) and alliance to displacement and warfare.
  • As colonial populations grew, competition for land intensified conflict.
  • Major conflicts: the Pequot War (1636–38) and King Philip's War (1675–78).

2.6

Slavery in the British Colonies

Key Points

  • Early Chesapeake labor relied heavily on indentured servitude.
  • Bacon's Rebellion (1676) exposed risks of a growing poor servant class.
  • Planters shifted to permanent, hereditary chattel slavery of enslaved Africans.
  • Slave codes formalized slavery as race-based, lifetime, and hereditary.
Bacon's RebellionChattel slaverySlave codes
Trap: Bacon's Rebellion is a cause of the shift to slavery, not a slave revolt itself.

2.7

Colonial Society and Culture

Key Points

  • The Great Awakening: religious revival emphasizing personal, emotional faith; challenged established church authority.
  • Enlightenment ideas (reason, natural rights) spread among educated colonists.
  • Growing print culture connected colonists across regions.
  • Colonial assemblies gave colonists direct self-government experience.

2.8

Comparison in Period 2

Key Points

  • Comparison: identifying similarities/differences between developments and explaining significance.
  • All regions relied on coerced labor at some point, but the scale varied enormously (family labor in New England vs. enslaved majority in the Lower South).
  • Religion varied sharply: Puritan New England vs. tolerant, diverse Middle Colonies.

⚠

Commonly Missed on the Exam

  • The triangular trade's three legs move in a specific direction — don't reverse them.
  • Bacon's Rebellion was a cause of the shift to slavery, not a slave uprising.
  • France's fur trade economy led to closer Native alliances than England's land-hungry settlements.
  • The Middle Passage refers specifically to the forced voyage of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic.
  • Mercantilism is the theory; the Navigation Acts are the specific enforcement laws.
  • Don't treat “colonial America” as one uniform society — regions differed sharply in economy, labor, and religion.
